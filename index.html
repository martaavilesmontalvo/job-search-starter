Decide what should put one role ahead of another.

Choose up to three priorities and give them weights that total 100%. Keep non-negotiable conditions in Must meet above; a high total cannot make up for a failed requirement.

Use your own priorities, such as work you want to do, relevant experience, compensation, growth or work arrangement. Define the same 1–5 scale for every role: 1 is weak but still acceptable, 3 acceptable and 5 strong for you; 2 and 4 fall between those anchors. Leave all rows empty if you want your assistant to propose a rubric for you to review first.

See how changing the weights changes the order

This fictional example uses illustrative priorities and judgments. The numbers are assumed for teaching; a real comparison needs the job description and your evidence.

Same role judgments, different priorities
CriterionRole A / 5Role B / 5First weightsChanged weights
Fit with experience and desired work4350%30%
Compensation3530%50%
Work arrangement5220%20%

First weights: A = (4 × .50) + (3 × .30) + (5 × .20) = 3.9 / 5. B = (3 × .50) + (5 × .30) + (2 × .20) = 3.4 / 5. A comes first.

Changed weights: A = 3.7 / 5; B = 3.8 / 5. Giving compensation more weight puts B first, using the same underlying judgments.

For a real rubric, define what those numbers mean for you before comparing roles. For example, a 5 for work arrangement could mean your preferred setup is confirmed; 3 could mean an acceptable compromise. An unknown arrangement stays unknown; it is not a 1 or a 0.

Repeat a search after it proves useful.

First inspect a few results: are the responsibilities relevant, which firm requirements are met, and what is unknown? Change a title, source or preference that brought back poor results. Then decide what to repeat.

Refine the search before repeating it. Share any useful or unsuitable results you have, or explain what was missing if none appeared. Explain which responsibilities or conditions matter. Ask AI to revise its search criteria and sources, not just reorder the same list.
